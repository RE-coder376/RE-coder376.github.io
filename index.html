Davis–Bacon Rate Lookup

2026 · Python, static site generation · public, free, no sign-up

Every current US federal construction wage determination, parsed from SAM.gov and republished as a county-level reference anyone can search. The government publishes these as PDFs that are almost unusable in practice; a contractor looking up the fringe rate for a single trade in a single county has to read a 60-page document. This turns that into one page per county per construction type.

I fetched all 4,009 determinations with two concurrent workers and zero failed documents, parsed each into structured rows, and generated the site. Then I submitted all 7,125 URLs through IndexNow and Search Console so the pages were actually findable rather than merely published.

QueueWatch — Past-Due Monitor for Action Scheduler

2026 · PHP, WordPress plugin · submitted to the WordPress Plugin Directory

WooCommerce runs its background work through Action Scheduler. When that queue stalls, orders stop emailing, subscriptions stop renewing, and nothing announces it — the site looks fine. QueueWatch watches for actions that are past due and tells the site owner before a customer does.

It shipped with 112 automated assertions run against a real WordPress and WooCommerce install rather than mocks. The review team returned it with three items — a naming conflict, function prefixes one character short of the guideline, and an ownership question — and I fixed all three in one pass, including a data migration that moves nine stored options by explicit name rather than by prefix sweep, because a sweep would have moved rows belonging to any other plugin using the same prefix.

QuoteRadar

2026 · Python, FastAPI, Meta WhatsApp Cloud API, Modal · deployed and working

Businesses that quote over WhatsApp lose money in a specific, boring way: a customer asks for a price and nobody replies. QuoteRadar sat on a company’s WhatsApp number, tracked every conversation’s state, and surfaced the ones waiting on a reply.

It ran live on Meta’s real infrastructure, not a sandbox — verified end to end with an actual message from my own phone arriving through the webhook, entering the state engine, and appearing on the dashboard. Authentication used signed HttpOnly session cookies with login throttling; the webhook stayed open to Meta and authenticated by X-Hub-Signature-256 instead, because Meta cannot log in.

Why it stopped

Connecting a real client’s existing WhatsApp number requires Meta’s Coexistence flow, and Coexistence requires Tech Provider status, which requires business verification, which requires a registered company. I do not have one. The only alternative was fully migrating a client’s number to the Cloud API, which permanently removes the WhatsApp Business app their staff work in every day. No real business accepts that. The product worked; the paperwork gate did not move, so I stopped rather than keep building against it.

Retrieval chatbot for Shopify and WooCommerce stores

2026 · FastAPI, ChromaDB, LangChain, HuggingFace embeddings, Modal · multi-tenant

A support and product-discovery bot embedded in online stores, answering from the store’s own catalogue with live price and stock rather than a stale index. It crawled each store behind quality gates, served a per-tenant embeddable widget with per-store branding, and included a CSS-only variant for pages that could not run JavaScript.

The part I am most pleased with is not the bot. It is the adversarial evaluation harness I wrote to attack it — deliberately hostile questions per store, scored automatically. Stores entered at 66% and I fixed engine bugs until most sat at 100%. It also generated its own demo videos by driving a headless browser and capturing real typing and streaming to MP4. Generation ran across three model providers with automatic failover, because a single provider deprecating a model mid-project had already taken the whole system down once.

Why it stopped

Over 100 stores and roughly 100 agencies contacted. Openers drew about 20% engagement, well above benchmark — and then every single conversation died at the moment I explained who I was. The diagnosis was trust, not wording: an unknown individual with no registered company asking a shop to put a script tag on their live storefront. Rewriting the message many times changed nothing, so I stopped rewriting and accepted the finding.

Certified payroll tooling — WH-347

2026 · Python · written against 29 CFR Part 5

Generates the US Department of Labor’s WH-347 certified payroll form from an existing payroll register, then verifies it: that each worker’s classification actually appears on the wage determination governing that contract, that hours split across classifications are reported per class per day rather than averaged, that fringe is tested per hour per classification, and that overtime across two classifications uses the weighted average regular rate — the calculation that most often fails audit.

Thirty automated checks, written from the regulation and the DOL’s own form instructions rather than from a blog post, with the separate certified-payroll requirements of all 50 states recorded alongside.

Data acquisition and verification pipeline

2026 · Python · concurrent scraping, structural validation

The scraping and verification layer underneath the outreach work. It finds companies, derives and checks their real domains, fetches every candidate page, and rejects the ones that do not hold up — then sends, with retries, and can never double-send because state is only written after a send returns.

The verification half exists because the naive version was confidently wrong. Guessing a domain from a company name produced other companies’ websites: a door-repair firm mapped to Automattic, a painting contractor to an online casino, a construction company to a foreign-exchange broker. Checking that a page merely contains a company word does not catch this. The domain has to be built from the company’s words in order, and then the live page has to be fetched and read.

AI Employee Vault

2026 · Python · agentic back-office with a human approval gate

An automated back office that runs on my own machine. Three watchers poll mail, an approvals folder and the filesystem; incoming email is classified by an LLM and routed; a planner drafts a reply and files it for approval. Nothing is ever sent by the system on its own — every outbound action waits on an explicit human approval, and the entry points refuse to run without a passing preflight check.

It is the system my MCP servers expose to Claude, which is why the assistant can operate my mail pipeline rather than describe what I should do in it. The approval gate is the part I would defend hardest: an agent that can act unsupervised on a real mailbox is a liability, and the correct design is one that drafts confidently and sends only on permission.

How I work with Claude

Claude is the environment I build in, not a tool I open

Everything above was built in Claude Code across roughly six months of daily use. Over that time I have built the scaffolding around it that makes long-running work possible — persistent memory, a house style, two agents that do not collide, and rules derived from my own mistakes rather than from advice.

A persistent, file-based memory

An indexed set of memory files — one per session or topic, with a lean index loaded at the start of every conversation. Decisions, measured numbers and pending steps survive between sessions, so a project picked up three weeks later starts from evidence instead of from my recollection.

A house style that overrides defaults

A CLAUDE.md that sets a plan / execute / revise loop, a hard output budget, banned filler words, and required preflight checks before any action that sends or posts anything. It is short on purpose, because a long one is not followed.

Two agents, one repository

A shared status file that Claude and Gemini both read before touching anything, listing which files are being modified. It exists because concurrent writes to the same JSON silently destroyed work — each process held a stale snapshot and deleted the other’s finished rows.

MCP servers for my own systems

Local MCP servers exposing my mail pipeline and accounting database as tools, so the assistant operates the systems I actually run rather than describing what I should do in them.

Rules written from real failures

A working-rules file that grows only when something goes wrong: never trust a marketplace search count without relevance-filtering it, never quote a deadline from a job that is serial rather than slow, never read a bounce rate from the inbox alone because bounces land in spam.

Render before done

Visual work is not finished until it has been rendered, captured and looked at. That rule earned itself — it caught a blank page, a progress bar pushing its own label onto a second line, and a settings row still describing behaviour the code had changed.

What the failures taught

Five products died in distribution, none in construction

I think this is the most useful thing I can tell you about myself, and it is the reason I would be a good ambassador rather than just an enthusiastic user. I have been wrong publicly and in writing, and I have kept the receipts.

  1. 01 Nothing I built failed because it did not work. Every one shipped, ran and was tested. They failed at the point where a stranger had to decide to trust an unknown individual with no company behind him. That is a distribution problem, and no amount of additional engineering touches it.
  2. 02 A green test suite is not evidence if the assertions cannot fail. I have written a tautological assertion and watched it pass while the system underneath it was completely down. Now I check that a test can fail before I trust that it passed.
  3. 03 Hypothetical interest is worth nothing. “That sounds useful” has never once become money. Eight people told me a product was interesting and none of them bought it. Only a request for commitment reveals anything.
  4. 04 Measure the instrument before believing the measurement. Marketplace search engines inflated counts by factors of ten to ten thousand; one reported a billion downloads for a term whose real figure was 72,050. Every count I now publish has been relevance-filtered first.
  5. 05 When the research converges, more research is procrastination. Five rounds of adversarial review across two different models produced one instruction: finish what you already built. Knowing when analysis has stopped adding information is a skill I learned the expensive way.
